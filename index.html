Module 1: Accounting Foundations from Absolute Zero

Source: Revision on Accounting Principles • Prerequisite: None

If you have never studied accounting before, start here. Accounting is the language of business. Every financial event is recorded in specific accounts using a double-entry rule.

⚖️ The Fundamental Accounting Equation
$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Equity}$$ In a partnership, Owner's Equity is expanded to represent multiple partners' capital accounts: $$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \sum (\text{Partner Capital Accounts})$$

Debit vs. Credit Nature of Accounts

In accounting, Debit (Dr) simply means the left side of an account, and Credit (Cr) means the right side. Accounts naturally increase on their home side:

Account Category Normal Balance To Increase (+) To Decrease (-) Examples in Course
Assets Debit Debit Credit Cash, Accounts Receivable, Inventory, Equipment, Furniture
Expenses & Losses Debit Debit Credit Depreciation Expense, Electricity, Salaries, Loss on Realization
Drawings (Withdrawals) Debit Debit Credit Drawings Account (represents personal withdrawals by partners)
Liabilities Credit Credit Debit Accounts Payable, Notes Payable, Partner Loans Payable
Capital / Equity Credit Credit Debit Ahmad Capital, Dina Capital, Common Stock, Retained Earnings
Revenues & Profits Credit Credit Debit Sales Revenue, Net Profit, Service Income
⚠️ Exam Trap: Profit vs. Revenue vs. Drawings
  • Revenue: The total gross money earned from delivering goods or services (e.g., $\$100,000$ in sales).
  • Profit (Net Income): The net amount left after deducting all expenses from revenue ($\text{Profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses}$).
  • Withdrawal / Drawings: Cash or assets taken out of the business by partners for personal use. A drawing is not a business expense; it reduces partner equity.

Module 2: Nature, Legal Definitions & Characteristics

Source: Definitions of Partnership & Characteristics

A partnership is formed when two or more persons unite their money, property, or skills to operate a business together.

Official Legal Definitions

  1. "By the contract of partnership, two or more persons bind themselves to contribute money, property, or industry to a common fund with the intention of dividing the profits among themselves. Two or more persons may also form a partnership for the exercise of a profession."
  2. "An association of two or more persons to carry on as co-owners of a business for profit."
  3. "A joint undertaking to share in the profit and loss."
  4. "A legal relation based upon the express or implied agreement of two or more competent persons whereby they unite their property, labor or skill in carrying on some lawful business as principals for their joint profit."

The 8 Core Characteristics

1. Ease of Formation

Formed by mere mutual agreement without elaborate corporate charters.

2. Separate Legal Personality

The firm has a distinct identity separate from the personal affairs of each partner.

3. Mutual Agency

Any partner can bind the partnership to business contracts within normal scope.

4. Unlimited Liability

General partners are personally liable for unpaid firm debts using their personal assets.

5. Co-ownership of Property

Assets invested become joint partnership property, not individual property.

6. Co-ownership of Profits

All partners share profits and losses according to their partnership agreement.

7. Limited Life

Admission, withdrawal, insolvency, or death of a partner dissolves the legal contract.

8. Consensual Transfer

A partner cannot transfer ownership to an outsider without unanimous consent.

Module 3: Partnership Formation (Brand New Firm)

Source: Scenarios 1: Assumptions 1, 2, 3 & Golden Co Example

When partners form a brand-new firm, they can contribute cash, non-cash property, or special services (industry).

📌 The 3 Cardinal Rules of Partnership Formation
  1. Agreed Value / Fair Market Value: Non-cash assets must be recorded at their fair market value or agreed value at the date of contribution, never at historical cost.
  2. NO Accumulated Depreciation Carried Forward: Accumulated depreciation from previous ownership is discarded. The new partnership records property at its agreed net value as the new historical cost.
  3. Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts IS Carried Forward: Accounts Receivable is entered at gross face value, and the agreed Allowance for Doubtful Accounts is credited to preserve customer records and show net realizable value.

Source: Menna & Dina (Star Co / DIM Co)

Method How it Works Key Journal Entries

Module 5: Partnership Operations & Profit Allocation

Source: Illustrations 1, 4 & Example 1

Unlike a sole proprietorship where all net income belongs to one person, a partnership must allocate net income through a Profit and Loss Appropriation Account.

📜 Order of Priority in Profit Allocation
  1. Interest on Partner Capital: Compensates partners for differing amounts of invested capital.
  2. Partners' Salaries: Compensates partners for personal time and management efforts.
  3. Partners' Bonuses / Commissions: Incentives based on net earnings.
  4. Interest on Drawings: Charged to partners who withdraw funds, credited back to the firm's appropriation account.
  5. Reserves: Transfers to general or statutory reserves.
  6. Remaining Divisible Profit / Loss: Divided strictly according to the agreed Profit and Loss Sharing Ratio. If no ratio is agreed upon, profits and losses are shared equally (50:50) by law.

Module 6: Advanced Managerial Commissions & Prior-Period Errors

Source: Illustration 4 (Partners A, B, and C)

One of the most heavily tested areas in university accounting is calculating partner commissions when agreements state "commission after charging..."

📐 The Commission Formulas

Case 1: Commission BEFORE charging such commission:

$$\text{Commission} = \text{Net Profit Base} \times \text{Rate}$$

Case 2: Commission AFTER charging his OWN commission:

$$\text{Commission} = \text{Net Profit Base} \times \frac{\text{Rate}}{100 + \text{Rate}}$$ Why? Because the commission itself reduces the base profit on which it is calculated: $\text{Commission} = 10\% \times (\text{Base} - \text{Commission}) \implies 1.10 \times \text{Commission} = 0.10 \times \text{Base} \implies \text{Commission} = \text{Base} \times \frac{10}{110}$.

Interactive Step-by-Step Solver: Illustration 4

Net profit reported: $162,000. Overlooked Plant Depreciation: $18,000. Reserve: 10%. Salary A: $2,000/mo. Commission terms: A gets 10% net profit; B gets 10% after A's commission; C gets 10% after A's, B's, and his own commission.

Step 1: Prior Period Correction
$$\text{Correct Net Profit} = \$162,000 - \$18,000 (\text{Depreciation}) = \mathbf{\$144,000}$$ Depreciation is an operating expense. It must be subtracted before calculating partnership appropriations.
Step 2: Reserve & Partner Salaries
  • Transfer to Reserve (10%): $\$144,000 \times 10\% = \mathbf{\$14,400}$
  • Salary to A: $\$2,000 \times 12 = \mathbf{\$24,000}$
  • Interest on Capital (10%): A: $\$6,000$, B: $\$8,000$, C: $\$5,000$ (Total $= \mathbf{\$19,000}$)
  • Interest on Drawings (10% on withdrawals): A: $\$400$, B: $\$600$, C: $\$300$ (Total Credit $= \mathbf{\$1,300}$)
Step 3: Calculating Partner Commissions
  • Commission A (10% of Net Profit): $$\$144,000 \times 10\% = \mathbf{\$14,400}$$
  • Commission B (10% after charging A's commission): $$\text{Base} = \$144,000 - \$14,400 = \$129,600 \quad \text{or course standard using 10/110}: \mathbf{\$13,091}$$
  • Commission C (10% after A's, B's, and HIS OWN commission): $$\text{Base} = \$144,000 - \$14,400 - \$13,091 = \$116,509$$ $$\text{Commission C} = \$116,509 \times \frac{10}{110} = \mathbf{\$10,592}$$
Step 4: Divisible Profit Distribution (5:3:2)

Total Credits ($144,000 + 1,300$) - Total Debits ($19,000 + 24,000 + 14,400 + 14,400 + 13,091 + 10,592$) = $49,817

Allocated per deed agreement: First $30,000 equally ($10,000 each), remainder $19,817 in ratio 5:3:2:

  • A: $10,000 + (19,817 \times 5/10) = 10,000 + 9,909 = \mathbf{\$19,909}$
  • B: $10,000 + (19,817 \times 3/10) = 10,000 + 5,945 = \mathbf{\$15,945}$
  • C: $10,000 + (19,817 \times 2/10) = 10,000 + 3,963 = \mathbf{\$13,963}$

Module 7: Fixed Capital System vs. Fluctuating Capital System

Source: Illustrations 2 and 3

How are partner earnings recorded in the ledger? Accounting provides two distinct frameworks:

Feature Fixed Capital System Fluctuating Capital System
Accounts Maintained per Partner Two: Capital Account AND Current Account. One: Capital Account only.
What is Recorded in Capital A/C? Only initial capital, permanent additions, or permanent withdrawals. The balance remains constant year-to-year. Everything: initial capital, drawings, salary, interest, and share of profit or loss.
What is Recorded in Current A/C? All recurring operational items: salary, interest on capital, share of profit/loss, and drawings. Not used (no current account exists).
Balance Sheet Presentation Shows Capital Account (fixed) $\pm$ Current Account balance. Shows fluctuating Capital Account ending balance.

Module 8: Partnership Dissolution (Bonus vs. Revaluation Method)

Official University Curriculum Core Topic

Dissolution is the change in the relation of the partners caused by any partner ceasing to be associated with the firm (e.g., admitting a new partner, retirement, or death). The business can continue under a new contract.

Admission by Direct Investment: Bonus vs. Revaluation (Goodwill)

Criteria Bonus Method Revaluation (Goodwill) Method
Total Contributed Capital (TCC) vs Total Agreed Capital (TAC) $\text{TAC} = \text{TCC}$. Total capital of the firm does NOT change. $\text{TAC} \neq \text{TCC}$. Total capital is adjusted upward to recognize unrecorded asset appreciation or goodwill.
Bonus to Existing Partners Occurs when New Partner Investment $>$ New Partner Capital Credit. The excess is credited to old partners in their P/L ratio. Assets revalued upward and credited to old partners in their old P/L ratio before admitting the new partner.
Bonus to New Partner Occurs when New Partner Investment $<$ New Partner Capital Credit. The deficiency is debited to old partners' capital in their P/L ratio. Implicit goodwill of the new partner is recognized and credited to the new partner's capital.
Bonus Method Formulation
$$\text{Bonus to Old Partners} = \text{Investment} - (\text{Total Agreed Capital} \times \text{New Partner's \% Interest})$$ If positive, bonus goes to old partners. If negative, bonus goes to the incoming partner.

Module 9: Partnership Liquidation (Winding Up of Affairs)

Official University Curriculum Core Topic

Liquidation is the final termination of the partnership enterprise. All business operations cease, non-cash assets are sold, liabilities are paid, and remaining cash is distributed to partners.

⚖️ The 4 Strict Legal Steps in Liquidation
  1. Realization: Sell all non-cash assets for cash. Record gain or loss in the Loss and Gain on Realization account.
  2. Division of Gain/Loss: Allocate the net gain or loss on realization among partners strictly in their P/L sharing ratio (never in capital ratio!).
  3. Payment of Liabilities: Pay all outside creditors and liquidation expenses. Outside creditors always have legal priority over partner capital.
  4. Final Distribution of Cash: Distribute the remaining cash to partners based strictly on their final positive capital balances.
⚠️ The Capital Deficiency Trap

If a partner suffers heavy realization losses that make their capital account negative (a debit balance), that partner is legally obligated to bring in personal cash to cover the deficiency. If the partner is insolvent, the remaining solvent partners must absorb the deficiency according to their relative P/L ratio.

Module 10: Corporation Accounting Foundations & Stockholders' Equity

Official University Curriculum Core Topic

A corporation is an artificial being created by operation of law, having the right of succession and powers authorized by law. Unlike partnerships, owners (shareholders) enjoy limited liability.

Structure of Stockholders' Equity

$$\text{Stockholders' Equity} = \text{Paid-in Capital} + \text{Retained Earnings} - \text{Treasury Stock}$$
Account Title Classification Normal Balance Accounting Purpose
Common Stock (Share Capital) Contributed Capital Credit Recorded strictly at Par Value or Stated Value per share issued.
Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par (Share Premium) Contributed Capital Credit Excess cash received over the par value when issuing stock.
Retained Earnings Earned Capital Credit Accumulated lifetime profits of the corporation less lifetime dividends paid.
Treasury Stock Contra-Equity Debit A corporation's own shares previously issued and later reacquired. Deducted from Total Stockholders' Equity.

Official Course Terminology & Formulas

Official Accounting Terms

Official Term Authoritative Definition Simple Explanation & Application
Profit and Loss Appropriation Account A temporary summary ledger account used by partnerships to distribute net profit among partners in the form of interest, salary, and profit shares. A special holding account that takes the net profit from the income statement and divvies it up according to the partnership deed.
Fixed Capital System An accounting method wherein partner capital accounts remain unchanged except for permanent capital injections or withdrawals. You keep two accounts for each partner: Capital Account (for the permanent investment) and Current Account (for regular profits, drawings, and interest).
Fluctuating Capital System An accounting system where all capital transactions, profits, salaries, and drawings are combined in a single partner Capital Account. Only one capital account is used per partner, so its balance changes (fluctuates) continuously throughout the year.
Bonus Method An accounting convention for partnership dissolution where total agreed capital equals total contributed capital, with any premium transferred between partner capital balances. No artificial goodwill is recorded on the balance sheet; existing partners or new partners simply grant each other a "bonus" from their capital.
Treasury Stock A corporation's own capital stock that has been legally issued, fully paid for, and later reacquired by the issuing company. Shares the company buys back from investors. Recorded as a contra-equity account (debit balance) that reduces total shareholders' equity.
Allowance for Doubtful Accounts A contra-asset valuation account associated with Accounts Receivable estimating uncollectible amounts. Carried over into the new partnership books to show customer balances at full face value while displaying estimated net cash collectibility.
